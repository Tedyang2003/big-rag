# Giant Kelp Restoration at Harrow Sound — Three Year Report

Prepared 30 September 2026 by the Harrow Sound Marine Trust.

## Background

Giant kelp held continuous cover across the eastern shelf of Harrow Sound until
the marine heatwave of February 2019, when water temperatures stayed above 19
degrees Celsius for 54 consecutive days. Surveys the following summer found
canopy cover reduced from an estimated 340 hectares to 29 hectares. The
understorey that remained was dominated by turf algae and by the purple urchin
Heliocidaris lacunosa, whose density rose from 1.2 to 14.6 individuals per
square metre over the same period.

Restoration began in October 2023 across four sites chosen to span the depth
and exposure range of the former forest: Kestrel Point at 8 metres, Minnow Bay
at 11 metres, Garnet Shoal at 14 metres, and Thimble Reef at 17 metres. Each
site received the same treatment sequence so that outcomes could be compared
without confounding the method.

## Method

Each site was divided into twelve plots of 25 square metres. Urchins were
removed by hand from eight plots at each site, with four plots left untouched as
controls. Removal continued monthly until density fell below 2 individuals per
square metre, which took between three and seven visits depending on site.

Juvenile kelp was raised in the Trust's hatchery from spores collected at the
two surviving patches on the western shelf, deliberately avoiding material from
outside the Sound so that local genetic structure was preserved. Spores were
settled onto twine, grown for 42 days, and the seeded twine was wrapped around
weighted lines laid across each cleared plot. Each plot received 40 metres of
seeded twine, equivalent to roughly 600 juvenile plants.

Monitoring used diver transects every three months, recording canopy cover,
stipe density, urchin density and the presence of understorey species. Water
temperature was logged continuously at each site at canopy depth.

## Results at Eighteen Months

Across all four sites, 62 percent of outplanted juveniles survived to eighteen
months. Canopy cover across the cleared plots reached 71 percent, against 4
percent in the untouched controls. Stipe density in the cleared plots averaged
9.4 per square metre, which is within the range recorded in archive surveys of
the intact forest from 2011 to 2016.

The figure of 62 percent conceals a sharp split by depth, and the explanation
lies almost entirely in urchin reinvasion rather than in anything about the
kelp itself. The two shallow sites, Kestrel Point and Minnow Bay, held urchin
density below 2 per square metre for the full eighteen months without further
intervention, and survival at those sites was 81 and 78 percent. The two deeper
sites did not. Garnet Shoal returned to 6.1 individuals per square metre within
seven months of the last removal visit, and Thimble Reef reached 9.8 within
five months. Survival at those sites was 49 and 38 percent.

Reinvasion at depth was traced to a continuous urchin barren extending south
from Thimble Reef across roughly 90 hectares of the outer shelf. That barren
was never treated, and it sits directly upcurrent of both deep sites during the
summer flow regime. Shallow sites are separated from it by a sand channel that
urchins cross only slowly. In other words the deep sites were not failing to
hold kelp; they were being continuously restocked with grazers from an
untreated area next door.

## Temperature

No site exceeded 19 degrees Celsius for more than 9 consecutive days during the
monitoring period. The warmest interval was 11 to 19 January 2026, when Minnow
Bay reached 19.4 degrees for six days. No bleaching or blade erosion was
recorded in that window, and survival at Minnow Bay was the second highest of
the four sites, so temperature is not treated as a limiting factor in this
report.

## Costs

Total project expenditure to 30 September 2026 was 1.84 million, of which 61
percent was diver time. Urchin removal accounted for 410,000 and hatchery
operations for 295,000. Cost per surviving plant at eighteen months was 128 at
the deep sites and 51 at the shallow sites, a difference driven entirely by
repeated removal visits and by lower survival.

## Recommendations

First, treat the outer shelf barren before extending outplanting to any site
deeper than 12 metres. Restoring a deep site while leaving its upcurrent barren
intact produces a plot that must be maintained indefinitely rather than one
that becomes self-sustaining.

Second, concentrate the next three years of outplanting between 6 and 12
metres, where the method demonstrably works without ongoing intervention.

Third, replace quarterly diver transects with annual transects supplemented by
towed camera surveys at the shallow sites that have exceeded 70 percent canopy
cover for two consecutive years. Diver time is the dominant cost and the
shallow sites are no longer changing quickly enough to justify it.

Fourth, retain the four untouched control plots at each site for a further
three years. They are the only evidence available that recovery does not occur
without urchin removal, and that evidence has already been requested twice by
the regional permitting authority.

## Limitations

The study has no replication across sounds, so nothing here establishes that
the depth pattern is driven by depth as such rather than by the particular
position of the Harrow Sound barren. Spore material came from two patches only,
and the genetic diversity of the outplanted population is therefore narrower
than the pre-heatwave forest. Finally, eighteen months is short relative to the
seven to nine year interval between heatwaves in the regional record, so no
claim is made here about resilience to the next one.
